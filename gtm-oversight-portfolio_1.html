Go to market with Initiatives

GTM teams invest in plays, training, and content to drive revenue — but have no reliable way to know if any of it is working. I designed the end-to-end product to close that gap.

Highspot is a B2B sales enablement platform used by enterprise GTM and revenue teams.

The Problem

Go to market teams are investing without visibility into what's working

Teams invest in developing materials such as training programs, plays, and conversation frameworks to support strategic initiatives, but have no reliable way to know whether any of it is actually working.

There is no reliable way to know whether reps are executing as expected, or whether those activities are driving revenue.

01 — Activity gap
No way to know whether reps are executing as expected.
02 — Outcome gap
No way to see impact of rep activity to revenue outcomes.
03 — Optimization gap
No way to decide what to fix, and what to scale.
The Product

Taking the product from 0→1

I led this project from 0 to 1. There was no PM on the project at the start, and context was scattered across go-to-market teams and the leadership team.

I used low-fidelity artefacts and design options as a tool for context gathering and alignment — surfacing assumptions, exposing disagreements between stakeholders, and driving decisions across teams.

01 — The Scorecard

Aligning GTM and leadership on what the scorecard should measure

The Initiative Scorecard was the most contested artefact in the product, so I started here.

I designed the first version of the scorecard with sections laid out as swimlanes so all four sections were visible above the fold. I organized them by intuition to gather early feedback. Over several rounds of review with GTM and leadership, I moved the design toward a causal hierarchy:

Enablement → Engagement → Business Drivers → Business Outcomes.

The scorecard renders this hierarchy top-down with Business Outcomes at the top, so customers read from result back to cause. This hierarchy became the organizing principle of the scorecard and shaped every downstream surface in the workflow.

02 — The Initiative Object

Designing the container that holds the content driving the initiative

Once the scorecard was settled, the question became what would hold the content that powered each initiative — the plays, training materials, conversation frameworks, and meeting topics GTM teams use to execute strategy.

I worked through end-to-end flow explorations to see how customers would create, populate, and manage an initiative across its lifecycle. The explorations covered information architecture options, the create flow, the overview surface, empty states, and how the initiative object would relate to existing objects in the product.

Explorations: end-to-end flow, IA options, create flow, overview, and empty states.

03 — The Final Product

A three-phase model for running a go-to-market initiative

The end-to-end flow consolidated into three phases that customers move through for any initiative. Each phase has its own surface, but all three connect through the Initiative object — context set in Define carries into Configure, and the metrics surfaced in Track tie back to the goals set in Configure.

This was an expansive project that evolved significantly from inception to general availability. The case study below presents two design pivots that were informed by customer feedback after the pilot release.

Customer feedback

Two major pivots between the pilot and the launch

The product was first released to a pilot cohort of 10 customers ahead of general availability. Feedback from that cohort surfaced two areas where the design needed substantial change, so the experience at launch reflects those pivots rather than the build the pilot customers used.

Pivot 01 — From blank canvas to a structured object

A blank-slate WYSIWYG editor made defining an initiative overwhelming. We replaced it with a structured drag-and-drop scaffold.

Pivot 02 — Creating a Business Drivers section for correlation metrics

The correlation metric was valuable but hard to understand. We introduced "Business Drivers" to shift the mental model and close the comprehension gap.

Pivot 01 — From blank canvas to a structured object

Blank slate experience increased the barrier to entry

The initial version of the Initiative creation experience was built on an existing WYSIWYG editor in the product. Users could drag and drop components to tell the story of the initiative and add relevant content.

Feedback from 10 customers in the pilot indicated that the open-ended format was not working — users focused on formatting decisions rather than the strategic content of the initiative.

7/10

Initiative owners said a blank slate was an overwhelming starting point

3/10

Did not expect to define the initiative in the WYSIWYG editor at all

4/10

Liked the WYSIWYG experience due to familiarity, but needed a template to reduce the number of decisions they had to make

I used that feedback to make the case for replacing the WYSIWYG with a structured drag-and-drop model. In the new structured object, users dragged and dropped relevant content to build the Initiative. The option to add a written overview was still available, but was no longer the default path.

BeforeOpen WYSIWYG — leaders design their own layout
AfterStructured drag-and-drop — constrained inputs, clear scaffolding, option to add an overview

Pivot 02 — Creating a Business Drivers section for correlation metrics

A naming decision that changed how the whole product reads

A key differentiator in the scorecard was a metric that correlated activity completion with revenue outcomes. Because revenue was the primary measure of practice success, I placed this metric in the existing Business Outcomes section of the scorecard.

Customer feedback highlighted that the metric was valuable but hard to understand:

0/10

Customers understood the metric before we explained it to them

10/10

Loved the metric once they understood it

Customer research highlighted that the data was useful but the product wasn't doing a great job of communicating this to the user. I had placed the metric in a "Business Outcomes" section, where the revenue figure was the headline and the activity driving that revenue appeared as secondary text below it.

In the short term, the team addressed the comprehension gap through customer onboarding conversations. Leadership discussions led to the introduction of a new "Business Drivers" section in the scorecardto host the correlation metric. The rationale was that the metric was measured using a business outcome, but was not itself a business outcome.

Introducing "Business Drivers" to change how customers read the data

The new name and a dedicated section in the scorecard gave the metric the emphasis and the space it needed to deliver a clear message: these are the activities with the highest impact on revenue.

Once activity became the primary lens for reading business drivers, the change had downstream implications across the product. To keep the message consistent throughout the experience, I updated the chart title and the chart configuration flow.
