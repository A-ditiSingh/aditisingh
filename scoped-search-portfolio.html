Making content findable for sellers in large orgs

Poor search relevance was the top reason enterprise customers rated Highspot negatively. I redesigned search to let reps scope results to content that's actually relevant to their role.

Role
Solo Product Designer
Timeline
Oct 2024 - Feb 2025
Team
1 Product Manager, 3 Engineers
Persona
Sales Representatives

Enterprise search experience in Highspot, a sales enablement platform that helps go-to-market (GTM) teams improve revenue growth by equipping, training, and coaching sales representatives.

Problem

Search relevance problems were putting the highest-value customer accounts at risk

Business problem
Poor search relevance was driving customer dissatisfaction
Approximately 70% of NPS detractors cited search relevance as their top reason for rating the product negatively.
The issue was most acute in larger, older accounts with high content volume — the same accounts that represented the greatest churn risk.
Search submission-to-click conversion rates in these accounts were lower than the average, indicating that users were not finding the items they searched for.
User Needs
Filtering facets did not match how reps thought about narrowing search results
Sales reps wanted to filter by high-level organizational constructs such as sub-org, region, and product line.
The system only supported more granular filtering facets that were tied to the spots and lists the items were added to.
The filter bar had too many facets, each too granular to be useful, and filter usage was very low.
Foundational work

The need for a higher-level filtering construct

  • Research identified a consistent rep behavior: when results weren't relevant, reps wanted to narrow by broad content areas — business units, regions, or product lines.
  • The platform had no way to associate content with these organizational groupings, so the filtering path didn't exist.

Content collection: a higher level grouping to match how reps search

Content collection

Designing a centralized method to tag content into groups that match how reps search

01

Admins create content collections that mirror how reps in their organization search — mapped to sub-orgs, product lines, or regions.

02

Admins assign the relevant spots (folders in the Highspot platform) to each collection.

03

When a publisher adds a piece of content to a spot, it is automatically tagged with the content collection that spot belongs to.

04

Reps limit their search results by content collection, surfacing only the content area most relevant to their role.

Content collections page in the admin settings

Early exploration — two interaction models

The filtering experience

Filtering results by content collections

  • I explored two models: scoping before submitting a query, and filtering post-search.
  • Scoping before submission in the search dropdown was an opportunity for quick and discoverable scoping.
  • When I designed the scoping experience for the maximum, it made the interaction very complex.
  • I used a working prototype to demonstrate the complexity and aligned the team on two principles:
    1. Submitting a scoped query should remain quick and simple.
    2. The search dropdown is high-value real estate and should not be cluttered.
Customer insight that shaped the design

Most sales reps will frequently engage with 1–3 collections

  • To keep pre-submission scoping simple, my PM partner and I returned to users with a focused question: how many collections do reps actually access when searching?
  • The data showed that even users with access to many collections consistently engaged with only 1–3.
  • This established the core design principle: make frequent tasks easy, and less frequent tasks possible.
1–3
Collections actively used by reps, even when many more were available
Key insight
Design implication of the customer insight

Limiting collections in the dropdown for simplicity, and deferring to filters for scalability

01Promote scoping to frequent collections — in the dropdown
The dropdown limits the scoping to 1–3 collections most relevant to the user directly. The dropdown stays clean.
02Allow filtering to other collections post-search
Defer to the filters for limiting results to rarely used content collections. This scales to edge cases.
Scoping queries in the search dropdown

Using the scoping requirement as an opportunity to modernize the search dropdown

  • The existing dropdown disappeared entirely while suggestions were loading, breaking the user's sense of continuity.
  • Adding scoping to the dropdown created an opportunity to redesign each state of the interaction for a more seamless experience.
  • I mapped the interaction into four distinct states and designed deliberate transitions between them, so the dropdown feels responsive throughout.
Redesigning each state of the search dropdown
01

Rest — no interaction has happened yet

The search bar is visible and discoverable.

02

Zero state — the user has clicked in

Intent is unknown. The dropdown surfaces recent searches to give the user a fast path to requerying.

03

Auto-suggest — the user is actively typing

The dropdown surfaces keyword and entity suggestions that update with every keystroke. Transitions need to be smooth for a high-quality experience.

04

End state — the user has submitted a query

The dropdown displays search query. The search box has quick paths for adjusting the query, changing the scope, or starting a new search.

Impact

Search to click conversion is higher when queries are scoped to content collections

  • Not every search needs to be scoped — a rep searching for a document by name has no reason to narrow scope.
  • The meaningful measure is whether scoping helps when reps choose to use it.
  • In organizations with content collections configured, scoped searches produce 15% more clicks on results than unscoped searches.
