The Problem Scorecards Were Created to Solve

A recurring need for visibility across the GTM team

The product contains multiple item types, each owned by different personas. A recurring need across the GTM team is visibility into whether any given item is actually meeting the objective it was created for.

Scorecards exist to answer that question. Today, nearly every item type in the product has one.

How It Started: The Original Five

Five scorecards, built through a two-SME collaboration

When the effort began in early 2025, five scorecards existed in the product. The working model was a two-SME collaboration: a Product Area SME (typically a PM) who understood the customer questions being asked, paired with an Analytics SME (also typically a PM) who knew the components, platform capabilities, and data availability. Design contributed by helping with components.

This model shipped the five scorecards, but it came with a cost. Each scorecard had been designed somewhat independently, which meant customers had to re-learn the structure every time they encountered a new one.

The Expansion Mandate and the New Question

Could a systematic structure sit on top of the existing components?

When the team was tasked with scaling beyond those five, the question shifted. Rather than continuing to build more scorecards the same way, I asked whether a systematic structure could be layered on top of the existing system of components — bringing consistency to what had previously been bespoke.

What We Knew About User Behavior

Personas use scorecards in two distinct modes

Two things were clear about how personas actually use scorecards. Most of the time they want a quick health check — if everything looks fine, they leave. When something is off, they need a clear path to drill down into the details.

The framework had to serve both modes without forcing one to carry the weight of the other.

mode 01
Quick health check
Persona opens the scorecard, scans the top-level data, and leaves if everything looks fine
mode 02
Drill-down
When a signal looks off, the persona needs a fast, predictable path into the detail behind it
The Proposed Structure

A framework designed around how personas actually read scorecards

The framework was designed around that behavior. Every scorecard would open with an overview tab built for the high-level check-in. Subsequent tabs would be organised around the specific questions customers ask, following identifiable high-level categories — training, content, people — that share a common structural pattern across item types.

Two principles tied the experience together
Principle 01
The overview tab maps directly to the subsequent tabs
Each section in the overview corresponds to a tab and surfaces the most important data from that tab. A persona can scan the overview, spot what's off, and know exactly where to go next.
Principle 02
Sections and tabs are ordered by walk-to-value
Both the overview sections and their corresponding tabs are ordered according to the walk-to-value for that scorecard, ensuring the most critical milestones in a customer's journey appear first.

How the framework reads

The overview becomes the map for the rest of the scorecard

A persona in check-in mode reads the overview top to bottom and sees the health of the customer journey in the order the customer walks it. A persona in drill-down mode sees the section that is off, reads the corresponding tab label, and moves straight into the detail.

The Result

Scorecards that feel familiar from the first encounter

The framework produced a predictable pattern customers can rely on regardless of which item type they are looking at. New scorecards plug into the structure rather than inventing a new one, and the two-SME working model still applies — Product Area and Analytics SMEs now contribute their questions and data into a framework that has already been validated against customer behavior.
