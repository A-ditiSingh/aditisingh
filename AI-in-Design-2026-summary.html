In 2025, designers were experimenting with AI. In 2026, they're rebuilding around it.

A condensed read of the second annual AI in Design report — 900+ designers surveyed across 60+ countries and 25+ qualitative interviews with design leaders. Three chapters: how toolstacks are shifting, how craft is being redefined, and how design orgs are racing to keep up.

01 · Tools

The great toolstack shakeup

AI usage has surged, but the stack itself is unstable. Designers are juggling double the tools, Claude has overtaken ChatGPT, coding tools are now first-class, and nearly half are still hunting for their go-to set.

78%
use Claude, overtaking ChatGPT (65%) as the primary general AI tool.
76%
have used an AI coding tool like Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, or Copilot.
74%
of designers at 2,000+ employee companies rely on internally-built AI tools (vs. 26% at small orgs).
01
AI is used end-to-end, not just in exploration
In 2025, AI lived in ideation and creation. Today every workflow shows meaningful adoption — code generation jumped +31 pts, wireframing +27 pts, design systems +24 pts.
02
Figma's role is shifting
Some treat it as a starting canvas, others as a "scalpel" for finishing work, and a code-first cohort barely opens it at all. Maze and Watershed designers now work directly in Cursor or the codebase.
03
Output quality is both the carrot and the stick
80% say reliable output is what makes a tool stick. 62% cite inconsistent output as their biggest challenge. The tools "aren't quite there" yet — but designers think they soon will be.
04
Designers are becoming toolmakers
Microtools, plugins, prompt libraries, internal "skills" — designers are encoding judgment and design systems into infrastructure. Anthropic open-sources internal tools across teams; Stripe ranks employee-built plugins on a leaderboard.

Key takeaways
01

The stack has doubled and turned fluid.

Most designers haven't settled on go-to tools and likely won't soon — every team, or every person, may run a different stack.

02

Designers are building their own software.

From microtools to agentic workflows, designers are encoding taste and process directly into infrastructure.

03

Enterprises route around adoption barriers by building internal tools.

This lets large design teams stay on the frontier without waiting on security and compliance.

04

Claude has overtaken ChatGPT.

Claude and Claude Code lead the General AI and Coding categories. Perplexity has collapsed from 34% → 13%.

05

Unreliable output quality is still the top blocker.

The software is vastly better than a year ago, but designers still call out inconsistency as the #1 challenge.

02 · Craft

Craft in an age of infinite output

Coding has gone from niche to baseline. Prototypes are an expected deliverable. Designers feel faster and more capable — and also more isolated, with concerns about cutting corners and craft atrophy showing up across every seniority level.

68%
of early-stage designers ship AI-generated code to production — vs. 33% at publicly traded companies.
70%
of designer-founders ship code — the highest of any role group.
2.5×
designers who code & prototype with AI are 2.5× more likely to feel more creative and capable.
01
Coding is now a core design skill
Half of all respondents have shipped to production — across brand and product, IC and exec. Only 20% identify as design engineers, showing how quickly the capability has spread beyond traditional technical roles.
02
Working prototypes are the new mockup
43% say functional prototypes are now expected; 36% say projects begin with one. They make user flows tangible — but also tempt teams to ship "alluring but underbaked" options.
03
Speed cuts both ways
85% say AI improved speed and efficiency. But code "forces commitment too early," compressing the open-ended phase where judgment develops. Leaders are trying to protect slow, divergent thinking time.
04
Designers still trust themselves over AI for craft
~80% rely on their own judgment for final polish, creative direction, and user empathy. AI assists; it doesn't replace the quality meter. 83% feel full or mostly-full ownership of AI-assisted work.

"It's like a burrito that took a minute and a half to microwave… It's easy to get enamored by a decent one-shot and overlook the flaws. Ask yourself: would I be happy with this if it took me a week to make?"

Katie Dill — Head of Design · Stripe

"There's loneliness replacing the collaborative energy. Waiting for AI to process replaces flow state. We can do cool things now, but everyone's building independently in a terminal."

Key takeaways
01

Coding is a designer's medium now.

Across disciplines and seniority levels — and a new collaboration surface with engineering.

02

Prototypes are a default output.

Teams are skipping static mockups and aligning around interactive flows earlier.

03

Speed unlocks creative range — and risks corner-cutting.

Designers are encoding quality into tools and rationing deep work for ambiguous problems.

04

Designers aren't handing over the keys.

80%+ still own craft, creative direction, and user empathy. AI is a "point-of-view amplifier."

05

Joy and anxiety coexist.

Most report higher satisfaction; many also describe craft atrophy fears, loneliness, and tool-churn fatigue.

03 · Teams

Redesigning the design org

Companies are investing more in AI adoption, but most learning still happens peer-to-peer. Roles are blurring between design, PM, and engineering — yet formal policies, performance metrics, and team structures are lagging behind the new expectations.

87%
receive at least moderate organizational support for AI adoption.
65%
say they're doing more PM and engineering work than before.
60%
of design leaders expect to keep or grow headcount in the next year.
01
Learning is bottom-up
Peer learning more than doubled (24% → 70%). Leadership recommendations halved (32% → 16%). The most-supported teams build cultures of tinkering, not playbooks.
02
Role lines are blurring in both directions
Designers are owning tickets end-to-end and writing code. 40% say their PMs and engineers are doing more design work. 34% say collaboration has become messier as roles get less clear.
03
Expectations are rising faster than policy
73% feel the bar rising. But only 28% of leaders have updated formal review metrics, career ladders, or hiring processes. Most orgs are still running 2024-era systems on 2026 expectations.
04
Hiring prioritizes AI fluency + systems thinking
50% of leaders are placing more emphasis on AI fluency, 48% on systems & strategic thinking. Craft is still essential — only 5% are placing less emphasis on execution polish.

Key takeaways
01

Designers learn more from peers than leadership.

The biggest unlock leaders can offer is space, time, and channels for knowledge sharing.

02

Design is becoming the connective tissue.

As more functions do "design work," designers are positioned as the holders of vision, empathy, and quality guardrails.

03

Official policy hasn't caught up.

Despite rising expectations, few orgs have updated review metrics, career ladders, or team structure.

04

Hiring is screening for strategy, taste, and adaptability.

AI fluency, systems thinking, storytelling — but craft fundamentals remain non-negotiable.

Looking ahead

Five open questions the next report will track

Where the research left off — the unresolved tensions worth watching through 2026 and into next year's edition.

Will we ever have a "go-to design stack" again? Or will every team — perhaps every designer — keep running a different, constantly-shifting set of tools?
What does craft mean when output is infinite? If visual polish is no longer hard, the bar may shift to storytelling, taste, and judgment — but the timeline isn't clear.
How do we design for agents, not just for humans? This surfaced rarely in the survey. The old interaction patterns won't apply to coworkers that aren't human.
Where do juniors learn the craft now? Entry-level roles have always been the apprenticeship into discipline and taste — and they may be the most AI-exposed.
Will more job titles include "AI"? AI design lead, AI design specialist, design architect — only 7% of teams have added them so far.
Does all this velocity produce better design, or just more of it? Faster shipping is the most visible change. Whether the quality floor is rising remains contested.
