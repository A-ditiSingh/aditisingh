## Section — The Problem

**Label:** The Problem

**Title:** Go to market teams are investing without visibility into what's working

**Body:** Teams invest in developing materials such as training programs, plays, and conversation frameworks to support strategic initiatives, but have no reliable way to know whether any of it is actually working.

There is no reliable way to know whether reps are executing as expected, or whether those activities are driving revenue.

---

## Section — The Product

**Label:** The Product

**Title:** Taking the product from 0 to 1

**Body:** I led this project from 0 to 1. There was no PM on the project at the start, and context was scattered across internal go-to-market teams and the leadership team.

I used low-fidelity artefacts and design options as a context-gathering tool. I used them to surface assumptions, expose disagreements between stakeholders, and drive alignment.

After several months of stakeholder and customer conversations, I defined the product around three phases of running an initiative:

**Three phases (chain):**

1. **Define** — Articulate the initiative and its strategic intent
2. **Configure** — Set goals and select the activities you want to measure
3. **Track** — Monitor progress through the Initiative Scorecard

**Body (continued):** The workflow gives teams the information they need to identify which activities to continue investing in and which to adjust.

This was an expansive project that evolved significantly from inception to general availability. This case study presents a few of the pivots that were shaped by customer feedback.

## Section — Customer feedback (summary)

**Label:** Customer feedback

**Title:** Two major pivots between the pilot and the launch

**Body:** The product was first released to a pilot cohort of 10 customers ahead of general availability. Feedback from that cohort surfaced two areas where the design needed substantial change, so the experience at launch reflects those pivots rather than the build the pilot customers used.

**Card 1 — Pivot 01 — From blank canvas to a structured object:** A blank-slate WYSIWYG editor made defining an initiative overwhelming. We replaced it with a structured drag-and-drop scaffold.

**Card 2 — Pivot 02 — Creating a Business Drivers section for correlation metrics:** The correlation metric was valuable but hard to understand. We introduced "Business Drivers" to shift the mental model and close the comprehension gap.

---

## Section — Pivot 01

**Label:** Pivot 01 — From blank canvas to a structured object

**Title:** Blank slate experience increased the barrier to entry

**Body:** The initial version of the Initiative creation experience was built on an existing WYSIWYG editor in the product. Users could drag and drop components to tell the story of the initiative and add relevant content.

Feedback from 10 customers in the pilot indicated that the open-ended format was not working — users focused on formatting decisions rather than the strategic content of the initiative.

**Data points (chain):**

- 7/10 — Initiative owners said a blank slate was an overwhelming starting point
- 3/10 — Did not expect to define the initiative in the WYSIWYG editor at all
- 4/10 — Liked the WYSIWYG experience due to familiarity, but needed a template to reduce the number of decisions they had to make

I used that feedback to make the case for replacing the WYSIWYG with a structured drag-and-drop model. In the new structured object, users dragged and dropped relevant content to build the Initiative. The option to add a written overview was still available, but was no longer the default path.

**Before / After:**

- Before — Open WYSIWYG — leaders design their own layout
- After — Structured drag-and-drop — constrained inputs, clear scaffolding, option to add an overview

## Section — Pivot 02

**Label:** Pivot 02 — Creating a Business Drivers section for correlation metrics

**Title:** The correlation metric was hard to understand.

**Body:** A key differentiator in the scorecard was a metric that correlated activity completion with revenue outcomes. Because revenue was the primary measure of practice success, I placed this metric in the existing Business Outcomes section of the scorecard.

Customer feedback highlighted that the metric was valuable but hard to understand:

**Data points (chain):**

- 0/10 — Customers understood the metric before we explained it to them
- 10/10 — Loved the metric once they understood it

**Body (continued):** Customer research highlighted that the data was useful but the product wasn't doing a great job of communicating this to the user. I had placed the metric in a "Business Outcomes" section, where the revenue figure was the headline and the activity driving that revenue appeared as secondary text below it.

In the short term, the team addressed the comprehension gap through customer onboarding conversations. In a leadership discussion, I proposed introducing a new "Business Drivers" section to host the correlation metric. The rationale was that the metric was measured using a business outcome, but was not itself a business outcome.

## Section — The Pivot (Naming & Framing)

**Label:** The Pivot — Naming & Framing

**Title:** Introducing "Business Drivers" changed how customers read the data

**Body:** The new name and a dedicated section in the scorecard gave the metric the emphasis and the space it needed to deliver a clear message: these are the activities with the highest impact on revenue.

Once activity became the primary lens for reading business drivers, the change had downstream implications across the product. To keep the message consistent throughout the experience, I updated the chart title and the chart configuration flow.

**Comparison table:**

|  | Business Outcomes | Business Drivers |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Model | Revenue is the primary lens | Activity is the primary lens |
| Chart reads | Avg. revenue: did vs. didn't | For this activity: revenue difference for did vs. didn't |
| Configure flow | Pick metric → pick activity | Pick activity → pick metric |

- Before — "Business Outcomes" framing (revenue is the primary lens)
- After — "Activity" framing (activity is the primary lens)

## Section — The Impact

**Label:** The Impact

**Title:** Initiatives had measurable impact across customer events, sales pipeline, and product adoption

**Timeline (chain):**

- **Oct '25 — Keynote demo** — The product was selected to demo at the opening keynote of Highspot's largest annual customer event
- **Jan '26 — Influenced pipeline** — Initiatives influenced 30% of active prospect pipeline — deals where buyers were actively discussing a Highspot purchase
